# Keep P/B, but adjust it before ranking

Raw cross-sectional P/B, the value signal in the TA's notebook, is the best-documented country value signal at a monthly rebalance. In Asness, Moskowitz & Pedersen, monthly B/M across 18 developed MSCI indices earned **6.0% a year long-short (Sharpe 0.61, t = 3.45, 1978–2011)**. Paired 50/50 with momentum, the Sharpe rose to **1.16** ([AMP 2013](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~lpederse/papers/ValMomEverywhere.pdf)). Two caveats weaken it for the notebook's setup. First, a broader 73-country horse race (1996–2017) puts **EBITDA/EV ahead of B/M**, with most of the premium coming from EM and frontier markets and fading over the last decade ([Zaremba & Szczygielski 2019](https://researchportal.northumbria.ac.uk/en/publications/and-the-winner-is-a-comparison-of-valuation-measures-for-equity-c/)). Second, raw P/B partly measures sector mix: bank-, energy- and materials-heavy markets look cheap, and tech- and pharma-heavy markets look expensive. No published paper tests a sector-adjusted country P/B out of sample. The case for adjusting rests on stock-level evidence, where industry-hedged value nearly **doubles HML's Sharpe (0.47 → 0.88)** ([Novy-Marx 2013](https://www.johnhcochrane.com/s/Novy_marx_OSoV.pdf)). For a 2-day project, the realistic choice is a value score made from three parts: raw P/B rank, an ROE- or sector-adjusted P/B residual, and long-run reversal. Each part is computed only from data available at t−1, and the score is always combined with momentum.

## P/B and CAPE lead, EBITDA/EV wins in broader samples

The ratios split cleanly by horizon. At **monthly** frequency, only B/M has a primary-source backtest on a universe like ours (MSCI and Bloomberg data, prior-month BE/ME). In that test the cheapest tercile earned **9.1% a year against 3.1%** for the most expensive ([AMP 2013](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~lpederse/papers/ValMomEverywhere.pdf)). Over **10–15-year** horizons, CAPE and P/B forecast about equally well. The cheapest decile earned about **13.3% (CAPE) and 13.6% (P/B)** a year in real terms, and trailing P/E, P/CF and dividend yield were "generally inferior" ([CXO on Keimling 2016](https://cxoadvisory.com/fundamental-valuation/comparing-cape-to-other-stock-market-valuation-ratios)). These tests are in-sample, with few independent windows. EBITDA/EV is the strongest result in the widest sample (**0.69% a month, Sharpe 0.81**, more than double B/M and dividend yield). That paper also reportedly finds that B/M performs poorly for country selection ([Alpha Architect summary](https://alphaarchitect.com/ebitda-to-ev-wins-again/)). We read only the abstract and summaries, so treat the B/M verdict as unverified.

| Signal | Best evidence | Horizon | Monthly Bloomberg build |
|---|---|---|---|
| P/B | AMP Sharpe 0.61; Keimling top decile 13.6% real | 1m and 10–15y | `PX_TO_BOOK_RATIO`, easy |
| CAPE | Keimling, ≈ P/B | 10–15y only | PX/PE → EPS, 120m average; EM only from ~2002 |
| Trailing P/E, dividend yield | Inferior to CAPE/P/B | long | Easy, but noisy (negative EPS) |
| EBITDA/EV | Best of 14 ratios, 73 countries | 1m | Field coverage on indices unverified |
| Composite | No direct country test | — | Average of z-scores |

P/B also suits cross-country use structurally. Its long-run "normal level" varies far less between countries (relative standard deviation **12.8% vs 24.7%** for CAPE), and it needs no 10-year smoothing ([Keimling 2016](https://gallery.mailchimp.com/6750faf5c6091bc898da154ff/files/Research_2016_01_Predicting_Stock_Market_Returns_Shiller_CAPE_Keimling_1_.pdf)). One implementation detail matters: use the current month's price in P/B. The "timely" version combines better with momentum ([Asness & Frazzini 2013](https://www.aqr.com/insights/research/journal-article/the-devil-in-hmls-details)).

## Sector bias is real, but no country-level fix has a published backtest

Practitioners use three adjustments. The first is **reweighting**: an implied P/B is built from the country's sector weights and world sector multiples, and the signal is log(actual ÷ implied). A blog test of this idea found US and ex-US forward P/Es nearly identical (~17.4x vs ~17.6x) once sector mix was equalised ([Fortune Financial Advisors 2017](https://fortunefinancialadvisors.com/?p=227)). The second is **sector-relative z-scores**, MSCI's own method for value indices, winsorised at ±3 ([MSCI Enhanced Value](https://www.msci.com/eqb/methodology/meth_docs/MSCI_Enhanced_Value_Index_Meth_Aug14.pdf)). The third is **like-for-like comparison** ("banks to banks"), which shrinks the Europe–US gap considerably ([Advisor Perspectives 2014](https://www.advisorperspectives.com/commentaries/2014/12/03/digging-deeper-for-market-valuations)). StarCapital wrote the only dedicated country paper ("Sector Adjusted Country Valuation", 2015), but it is offline and its method and results are unknown. The stock-level analogue is strong. Most of B/M's predictive power is within-industry ([Asness, Porter & Stevens 2000](https://www.aqr.com/Insights/Research/Working-Paper/Predicting-Stock-Returns-Using-IndustryRelative-Firm-Characteristics)), and hedging industry exposure lowers the mean return while raising the Sharpe ([Novy-Marx 2013](https://www.johnhcochrane.com/s/Novy_marx_OSoV.pdf)). Applied to countries, the expected gain from adjusting is **lower volatility and a better IR, not a higher return**.

Not all of a P/B gap is sector noise. Part of it reflects real profitability: the US ROE premium is "not merely a reflection of sector composition" ([Cambridge Associates 2019](https://publishedresearch.cambridgeassociates.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/VantagePoint-Q3-2019.pdf)). The cheapest workable fix is therefore a monthly cross-sectional regression of log P/B on ROE, keeping the residual. Justified P/B rises with ROE, and high-P/B sectors are high-ROE sectors, so the residual absorbs much of the sector mix without needing country × sector data.

## Own-history z-scores remove level bias but break when the index changes

An own-history z-score removes each country's persistent premium (stable sector mix, accounting, ROE) and is trivial to compute. It fails in exactly the cases that matter most for sector bias. **MSCI Denmark's health-care weight rose from ~10% to ~60%** as its constituents fell to 11. Dropping markets with large sector shifts lifts the pooled CAPE→return R² from **0.49 to 0.58** ([Keimling 2016](https://gallery.mailchimp.com/6750faf5c6091bc898da154ff/files/Research_2016_01_Predicting_Stock_Market_Returns_Shiller_CAPE_Keimling_1_.pdf)). So for markets like Denmark, the Netherlands or Taiwan, "own history" describes a different index. Practitioners still prefer own-history comparison for CAPE ([Monevator](https://monevator.com/cape-ratio-by-country/)), but this is opinion, not a test. No paper we found compares own-history z-scores with the raw cross-section for monthly country selection, so a 50/50 blend is a defensible hedge rather than a proven improvement. The z-score must use a **rolling 60m (or expanding) window ending at t−1, with at least 36 months of data**. A full-sample mean and standard deviation is look-ahead.

## Long-run reversal is a price-only value proxy

National indices mean-revert relative to each other with a **half-life of 3–3.5 years** ([Balvers, Wu & Gilliland 2000](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jfinan/v55y2000i2p745-772.html)). Loser countries outperform winners over 3–5 years without being riskier ([Richards 1997](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jfinan/v52y1997i5p2129-44.html)). Across more than a century of international data, the effect is not subsumed by value ([Zaremba, Kizys & Raza](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/436972/)). AMP's −5-year-return value factor correlates **0.86 with B/M** globally in stocks ([AMP 2013](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~lpederse/papers/ValMomEverywhere.pdf)). It needs only `PX_LAST`, so it fills gaps in EM book data. Skip the latest 12 months so it does not mechanically offset 12-1 momentum.

## Conclusion

The notebook's raw P/B is a reasonable starting point, not a mistake. Its weakness is a sector bet that adds volatility without clear reward, and the evidence for that comes by analogy from stocks rather than from country backtests. With 21 countries and a top-4 long-only portfolio, any backtest has a very short effective sample. Judge variants by monthly rank IC and its t-stat, not by one equity curve, and report how many variants were tried, because each extra signal tested raises the bar for any winner. The post-2006 decay in country value means a modest premium is the honest expectation.

**Recommendation (2-day project).** (1) Build value = mean of the cross-sectional z-scores of −log P/B and the ROE-residual P/B (reversal only as a separate variant, given the post-2005 evidence in the follow-up below), and combine it 50/50 with 12-1 momentum before taking the top 4. (2) Use only rolling or expanding statistics ending at t−1, lag fundamentals by one month, and use no full-sample z-scores or current sector weights. (3) Cap the trials at about 5 variants (raw P/B, own-history z-score, ROE-residual, reversal, composite), pre-register the composite as the main result, and report all 5 ICs.

## Follow-up: recent reversal evidence and the P/B-ROE model

The reversal leg is weaker than the section above implies. Over 1830–2019, losers beat winners and value does not subsume the effect, but the authors call it "highly unstable through time" ([Zaremba, Kizys & Raza 2020](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/436972/)). Recently it has flipped. Across 74 MSCI markets in 1999–2015, losers minus winners on t−60 to t−13 returns earned **−1.22% a month (t = −2.36)**, and −1.09% after country value, size and momentum. Developed markets alone gave −0.47% (t = −1.58) ([Zaremba 2016](https://ideas.repec.org/a/rjr/romjef/vy2016i1p88-103.html)). Across 64 countries, the reversal alpha fell from about **1.2% a month in 1976–90 to between −0.2% and −0.5% in 2005–18**, and country value fell to about 0.03% ([Zaremba & Umutlu 2020](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jbfina/v121y2020ics0378426620302284.html)). So reversal should be a reported variant, not a third of the composite.

The ROE-residual P/B follows Wilcox ([FAJ 1984, 40(1):58–66](https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/en/research/financial-analysts-journal/1984/the-pb-roe-valuation-model); Wilcox & Philips, *JPM* 2005, 31(4):56–66). Regressing ln(P/B) on ROE explains about **26% of US stock P/B, up to 50% for stable-ROE firms** (Value Line 1988–2002). That is price fit, not return prediction. The authors warn a good price model "may not forecast returns", and we found no published backtest of the residual. The closest country test, a residual-income model on 19 developed markets (1988–2005), found significant returns ([Desrosiers et al. 2007](https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/en/research/financial-analysts-journal/2007/residual-income-approach-to-equity-country-selection)), but it ends before the post-2005 decay and we saw only the abstract. Treat the residual as a sector adjustment, not proven alpha. Details: `research_notes/Country value signals sector bias/reversal_wilcox_followup.md`.
