01 — Market outlookRound 1

Market outlook

Our reading of the macro picture is that 2026 has been a year of stagflation, that economists expect a return to goldilocks in 2027, and that markets do not share this optimism. Fed funds futures, in fact, price about 3.4 further hikes, reaching roughly 100 basis points above what economists forecast. Hence, we do not build the portfolio around a single regime, but hold assets that perform in each of them.

1.1 · Growth and inflation

We classify each economy into the four regimes defined by the direction of growth and inflation, using the Bloomberg consensus forecasts. During 2026 inflation rose in every region, in the United States and in the emerging economies it did so while growth was slowing: US CPI went from 2.7% to 3.4% and real GDP growth fell from 2.6% to 1.8%, which places both economies in stagflation. For 2027, the consensus expects growth to remain steady or to rise and inflation to fall in every region, the pattern of a goldilocks economy. [1]

03 — The ETFs3.1 Equity

The ETFs and why we chose them

3.1 · Equity

Equity carries the equity risk premium, the most reliable source of long-run excess return. Instead of a single world ETF we hold three regional blocks: this gives us control over the regional mix and makes our outlook visible in the portfolio.

VTIVanguard Total Stock Market ETF
US equity · CRSP US Total Market Index · listed May 2001 · 3,531 holdings · TER 0.03% (30 Jun 2026) [5]
Why we chose itThe US is the deepest and most innovative equity market: it represents 64% of the MSCI ACWI [6]. US companies have delivered the strongest earnings growth of the last decade, driven by technology and, more recently, the investment cycle in artificial intelligence, visible in the strength of private investment. US equity is therefore the main source of expected return in our portfolio.
Why not SPY / VOOSPY and VOO hold the 500 largest companies; VTI holds about 3,500, adding mid and small caps that are more sensitive to domestic growth and to falling rates, and slightly less concentrated in the largest technology names.
Risks we acceptHigh valuations and concentration in mega caps; with the 10-year yield at 5.24% bonds are a serious alternative, which compresses the equity risk premium. We limit these risks with the concentration cap and by holding equity outside the US.
VEAVanguard FTSE Developed Markets ETF
Developed ex-US equity · FTSE Developed All Cap ex US Index · listed July 2007
Why we chose itWe do not want the portfolio to depend on one economy. Developed ex-US markets (Europe, Japan, UK, Canada, Australia, South Korea) trade at a valuation discount to the US and have a different sector mix — more financials, industrials and healthcare, less technology — so they respond differently to the same shocks. The fund is unhedged: the expected weakening of the dollar (EUR/USD +3.5% to end-2027) would add to its returns in USD.
Why not EFA / VXUSEFA excludes Canada and small caps; VEA includes both. We preferred VEA to an all-in-one ex-US fund such as VXUS to size emerging markets separately.
Risks we acceptWith European growth expected at only 1.3–1.6%, the case for this position rests on valuations rather than on earnings momentum, and it may take time to pay off. Many European and Japanese companies are large exporters, which leaves them exposed to tariffs and trade tensions, and since the fund is unhedged, a stronger dollar would reduce its value in USD.

Why we chose itEmerging markets offer the best macro combination of the three regions: growth rising from 3.9% to 4.4% in 2027, inflation falling and central banks easing by about 140 bp [1], with current-account surpluses of about 3% of GDP that reduce their dependence on foreign capital. 3.2 · Fixed income

Bonds lower the volatility of the portfolio, which is necessary to stay within the 7–9% risk budget, and protect it in a recession with falling inflation. We hold three different sources of fixed-income return: US nominal rates, non-US rates and US real rates.

BNDVanguard Total Bond Market ETF
US investment-grade bonds · Bloomberg US Aggregate Float Adjusted Index · listed April 2007
Why we chose itBND holds the US investment-grade market: Treasuries, agency mortgage-backed securities and IG corporate bonds, with an intermediate duration. With the 10-year yield at 5.24% and the 2-year at 4.80%, bonds pay a meaningful income; economists expect the 10-year yield to fall to about 4.6% by end-2027 [1], which would add a capital gain. Above all, BND is our insurance against a recession: when growth and inflation fall, central banks cut and bond prices rise.
Why not TLTThe US budget deficit is expected at about 6.5% of GDP and public debt at 105% of GDP [1]: a persistent supply of Treasuries may keep long-term yields high. Intermediate duration captures most of the recession hedge with much less of this risk. We excluded high-yield bonds: with a spread of 3.15% they pay little for a credit risk that rises exactly when equities fall.
Risks we acceptSince 2021 the stock–bond correlation has turned positive: when inflation is the problem, bonds and equities can fall together, as in 2022. Futures also price further Fed hikes.

3.4 Risk-management tool · US Treasury bills, 1–3 months · listed May 2007
RoleBIL is not a strategic position but a tool to manage the risk budget. If the eight strategic ETFs together have an ex-ante volatility above 9%, we scale the risky portfolio down to the 8% target and invest the remainder in BIL; if the volatility is already within the 7–9% band, BIL is not held at all. Why not SGOVSGOV is an equivalent and slightly cheaper alternative; we use BIL because its longer track record (2007, against 2020 for SGOV) gives more data if the backtest sample is extended.

4.1 · What the correlations tell us

Each basket behaves almost as one asset. The three equity ETFs correlate 0.72–0.82 and the three bond ETFs 0.85–0.90, so there is little diversification inside a basket. Diversification comes from combining the baskets, which is why our method sets the risk of each basket (section 05).

Stocks and bonds have moved together. Their correlation is 0.47–0.64 over a window that includes 2022. Bonds still lower the volatility, but they hedge equities less than they did before 2021 (Class 3, Part II). For this reason we do not rely on bonds alone for protection.

Gold and commodities are the real diversifiers. Their correlations with the other ETFs are at most 0.47, and commodities are uncorrelated with bonds (−0.13 to 0.12). This is the statistical side of the role that we give to real assets in section 03.

4.2 · Why we do not use the expected returns

The historical excess returns describe the last five years, not the next ones. Bonds show −4% a year because yields rose sharply in 2022, the same rise that now gives bonds a yield above 5%. Gold shows +14.6% because of a record rally. With five years of data, the standard error of the mean of an ETF with a 15% volatility is 15% / √5 ≈ 6.7 percentage points, larger than most of the means themselves. An optimiser using these numbers puts a third of the capital in gold and nothing in bonds (section 05). Our weights therefore depend only on the volatilities and the correlations, which are estimated much more precisely.

05 — MethodRound 1

The method: risk budgets by basket

Our weights come from a two-level risk-budgeting rule that uses only the covariance matrix. Inside each basket every ETF carries the same share of the basket's risk (equal risk contribution, ERC). Across the baskets the risk is split 50% equity, 25% bonds, 25% real assets. The rules of the round apply: long-only, no leverage, ex-ante volatility between 7% and 9%.

5.1 · How the weights are computed
Step 1 · inside the basketERC weights, so that wi(Vw)i / w′Vw is the same for every ETF of the basket. Unlike 1/σ, ERC uses the correlations.
Step 2 · across basketsEach basket becomes one return series. On their covariance matrix we solve min Σk(%RCk − bk)², with b = 50 / 25 / 25, w ≥ 0, Σw ≤ 1 and 7% ≤ σ ≤ 9%.
Step 3 · combineETF weight = basket weight × weight inside the basket; whatever is not invested goes to BIL. Solved numerically (SLSQP, scipy).
5.2 · Why a risk-based method

Estimation error. Mean–variance turns small errors in expected returns into extreme weights. In the 40-year horse race of Class 3, its out-of-sample Sharpe Ratio fell to 0.04, against 0.53 for risk parity. Our inputs (section 04) show the same problem: historical means are both noisy and backward-looking. A rule that needs no expected returns avoids it.

Regimes. Equities pay in goldilocks, bonds in a recession with falling inflation, real assets under inflation (Class 3). Our three baskets map onto these regimes, and fixing their risk shares means that no single regime can dominate the result. This fits our outlook: the next regime is uncertain (section 01).

Capital is not risk. A 60/40 portfolio has about 90% of its risk in equities. By budgeting the risk directly we control what each basket can do to the portfolio, whatever its capital weight.

5.3 · Why 50 / 25 / 25

Equal risk across the baskets would be the maximum-Sharpe portfolio only if the baskets had the same Sharpe Ratio and the same correlations [15]. Long-run evidence says they do not: the equity premium is the largest and best-documented premium, and it is backed by cash flows [16], while gold has none and the commodity premium is debated. We therefore split the risk 50/50 between growth and protection, and the protection equally between bonds and real assets. This is a prior on the ranking of the premia, not an estimated return.

It is also the budget closest to the 8% target. Out of sample, the four budgets we tested have practically the same Sharpe Ratio (Table 5), so a larger equity budget would add volatility but no risk-adjusted return.

6.1 · Capital versus risk

Bonds take 45% of the capital but only 25% of the risk; equity takes 31% of the capital and 50% of the risk. A dollar in equity moves the portfolio about 2.5 times as much as a dollar in bonds (volatility 15–16% against 6–7%). The capital weights therefore look defensive, but the risk is split evenly between growth and protection, by design.

6.2 · Inside the baskets

VEA and IEMG get the same weight although IEMG is more volatile (16.3% against 15.7%), because IEMG is less correlated with VTI (0.72 against 0.82). In the whole portfolio, however, VEA and IEMG contribute more risk than VTI, because they are more correlated with bonds and gold. BNDX has the largest weight because it has the lowest volatility (5.6%). Between the two real assets, BCI gets more capital because it is less volatile; it also contributes less risk than gold (9.5% against 15.5%), because it is the least correlated asset of the portfolio.

6.3 · Volatility and Value at Risk

The ex-ante volatility, √(w′Vw) = 8.13%, is inside the band and close to the target. Under normality, the 1-month 5% VaR is 1.645 × 8.13% / √12 = 3.86%: in one month out of twenty we expect to lose more than USD 3.86 million. We read this as a reference, not a ceiling. In the backtest the worst month of these weights was −9.4% (March 2020), and the monthly returns are skewed to the left, so the true tail is fatter than the normal one. Realised volatility can also differ from 8.1%: the window includes 2022, and in calmer years such as 2013–2021 the same weights had a volatility of 6.9%.

07 — BacktestRound 1

The backtest

We hold the SAA weights fixed and rebalance them to target at the end of every month; whatever is not invested earns the BIL return. The sample is July 2013 – September 2026 (159 months): it starts when every ETF exists, the limit being BNDX (June 2013). There are no transaction costs, since the guidelines do not mention them and monthly rebalancing only trades the drift of the weights. The measures are those of Class 2. Because the weights are estimated on the last 60 months, a single backtest would partly test the weights on the data that produced them. We therefore run four versions:

Full sampleOur weights on all 159 months. The last 60 are in-sample.
Hold-outOur weights on the 99 months before the estimation window, which the optimiser never saw.
SplitThe rule estimated once on the first 60 months, then held fixed on the following 99. No look-ahead.
Walk-forwardThe rule re-estimated every month on the previous 60 months, as an investor would have done in real time.

7.2 · Reading the results

Return and risk. Over the full sample, USD 1 grew to USD 2.11: 5.9% a year with 7.4% volatility and a Sharpe Ratio of 0.55. The portfolio lost money in three of the twelve full calendar years (2015, 2018, 2022), never more than 9.4%.

2022, the stress test that matters. From October 2021 to September 2022 the portfolio lost 13.0%, its maximum drawdown. In 2022 US equity fell 20% and US bonds 12.5%, so the usual hedge failed. Commodities (+15.7%) and gold (+0.8%) were the only positions that gained, which is the regime for which we hold them. The loss was recovered by March 2024. In March 2020 (−9.4% in the month, −10.9% from the peak) equities fell about 20% and commodities 17%, while bonds and gold held their value; the loss was recovered by July.

Volatility depends on the regime. With the same weights, realised volatility was 6.9% in 2013–2021, when stocks and bonds were negatively correlated, against 8.1% in the estimation window. Our weights are calibrated on the recent, less favourable regime, so they should not exceed the band if that regime persists.

The evidence is statistically weak. The t-statistics of the mean excess return are between 1.4 and 2.0: none of the tests proves at the 5% level that the SAA beats the T-bill. This is the Class 2 lesson that decades of data are needed, and it is why Checkpoint 3 discounts the backtested Sharpe Ratio.

Where the rules disagree with our weights

The three allocations share the same shape: every ETF holds 7–22% of the capital, all within the 7–9% band. They disagree in three places. Equity: equal weight gives it 37.5% of the capital and 56% of the risk, because it ignores that equities are 2.5 times as volatile as bonds; risk parity gives it 24% and 42%. Our 31% of capital and exactly 50% of risk lies in between, and it is the one choice in our weights that rests on a view about returns. That view is a prior that the equity premium is the most reliable premium, not an estimated mean. Bonds: risk parity puts 61% of the capital in the three bond ETFs because each looks safe on its own. But they are correlated 0.85–0.90 with each other and, since 2021, positively with equities, so 1/σ counts the same risk three times. Our ERC uses the correlations and holds 45%. Real assets: risk parity cuts gold and commodities to 15% of the capital and 16% of the risk because they are volatile on their own. It ignores that they are the least correlated assets in the universe and the only ones that gained in 2022. We keep them at 25% of the risk, on the basis of correlations and our regime analysis, not their past returns. None of our weights depends on an estimated expected return. The positions that would — 33–35% in gold under maximum Sharpe and mean–variance — are exactly the ones we rejected. Out of sample, no rule dominates: risk parity wins the hold-out (Sharpe 0.78 against our 0.66), equal weight the walk-forward (0.61 against 0.48). We therefore choose on economic grounds: we prefer the rule that gives each regime a fixed share of the risk.

4 · The discount
Backtested Sharpe (hold-out)0.66

We start from the hold-out, the only test of our own weights on months they were not estimated on, and halve it, following the rule of thumb of Class 4. A raw 0.66 would overstate what we expect, for five reasons. (1) With 37 variations, some look good by chance alone. (2) The hold-out was consulted when we chose the budget. (3) The ETFs were chosen in 2026, knowing for example that gold rallied. (4) In 2013–2021 rates were falling and stocks and bonds hedged each other, which no longer holds. (5) There are no trading costs. The t-statistic of 1.9 also means a standard error of about 0.35 on the Sharpe Ratio. We do not discount more because our weights have a single tuned parameter, the equity budget, and use no expected returns. The value of 0.33 is consistent with the evidence that does not depend on our choices: our walk-forward gave 0.48, and in the 40-year horse race of Class 3 risk parity earned 0.53 out of sample, and a 60/40 portfolio has a Sharpe Ratio of 0.36.
