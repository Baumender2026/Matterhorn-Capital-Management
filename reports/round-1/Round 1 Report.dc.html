01 — Market outlookRound 1

Market outlook

Our reading of the macro picture is that 2026 has been a year of stagflation, and that economists expect a return to goldilocks in 2027. Markets agree that inflation will fall, but not on how: economists expect the Fed to cut as inflation falls, while fed funds futures price about 3.4 further hikes, as if inflation will fall only because the Fed keeps rates high. The two paths favour different asset classes, and we cannot tell which one we are on. Hence, we do not build the portfolio around a single scenario, but hold assets that perform in each of them.

1.1 · Growth and inflation

We classify each economy into the four regimes defined by the direction of growth and inflation, using the Bloomberg consensus (ECFC). Between Q1 and Q3 2026 inflation rose in every region. In the United States and the emerging economies growth slowed at the same time, which places both in the stagflation quadrant (growth slowing, not contracting): US CPI rose from 2.7% to 3.4% and real GDP growth fell from 2.6% to 1.8%. In the other developed economies growth rose as well, so they are in the overheating quadrant. For 2027, the consensus expects inflation to fall back to 2–3% in every region, with growth broadly steady: a return to goldilocks in the United States and the emerging economies. [1]

Monetary policy: economists versus markets

After the September hike the Fed funds target range stands at 3.75–4.00%, and the path of the policy rate is where economists and markets disagree most. Economists expect one further hike followed by cuts that would bring the rate to 3.85% by early 2028. Markets expect the opposite: fed funds futures, whose prices reflect the rate investors expect, imply a rate of about 4.84% by the end of 2027, roughly three to four further quarter-point hikes. 03 — The ETFs3.1 Equity

The ETFs and why we chose them

Each ETF has a role in our strategy: equity is the growth engine; bonds are the brake when inflation falls, real assets when it rises. BIL is a tool to manage the risk budget, not a strategic position.

3.1 · Equity

Equity earns the equity risk premium, the best-documented source of long-run excess return. We hold three regional ETFs weighted by risk, not by market capitalisation. A market-cap allocation would be concentrated: 64% in the US (MSCI ACWI [6]), and much of it in a few AI-linked mega caps, as VTI’s top ten holdings are 36.1% of the fund [5]. Weighting by risk limits the US share: the US is 35% of our equity.

Bonds are the brake when inflation falls: in a recession with falling inflation, central banks cut and bond prices rise. They also lower the volatility of the portfolio, so that it stays within the 7–9% budget. We hold three sources of return: US nominal rates, non-US rates and US real rates.

4.1 · What the correlations tell us

Each basket behaves almost as one asset. The three equity ETFs correlate 0.72–0.82 and the three bond ETFs 0.85–0.90, so there is little diversification inside a basket. Diversification comes from combining the baskets, which is why our method sets the risk of each basket (section 05).

Stocks and bonds have moved together. Their correlation is 0.47–0.64 over a window that includes 2022. Bonds still lower the volatility, but they hedge equities less than they did before 2021 (Class 3, Part II). For this reason we do not rely on bonds alone for protection.

Gold and commodities are the real diversifiers. Their correlations with the other ETFs are at most 0.47, and commodities are uncorrelated with bonds (−0.13 to 0.12). This is the statistical side of the role that we give to real assets in section 03.

4.2 · Why we do not use the expected returns

The historical excess returns describe the last five years, not the next ones. Bonds show −4% a year because yields rose sharply in 2022, the same rise that now gives bonds a yield above 5%. Gold shows +14.6% because of a record rally. With five years of data, the standard error of the mean of an ETF with a 15% volatility is 15% / √5 ≈ 6.7 percentage points, larger than most of the means themselves. An optimiser using these numbers puts a third of the capital in gold and nothing in bonds (section 05). Our weights therefore depend only on the volatilities and the correlations, which are estimated much more precisely.

05 — MethodRound 1

The method: risk budgets by basket

Our weights come from a two-level risk-budgeting rule that uses only the covariance matrix: equal risk per ETF inside each basket (ERC) and, across the baskets, 50% equity, 25% bonds, 25% real assets. Long-only, no leverage, ex-ante volatility between 7% and 9%.

5.1 · How the weights are computed
Step 1 · inside the basketERC weights, so that wi(Vw)i / w′Vw is the same for every ETF of the basket. Unlike 1/σ, ERC uses the correlations.
Step 2 · across basketsBasket weights that bring each basket’s share of risk closest to 50 / 25 / 25, with w ≥ 0, Σw ≤ 1 and 7% ≤ σ ≤ 9%.
Step 3 · combineETF weight = basket weight × weight inside the basket; BIL takes the rest only when the 9% cap binds. Solved numerically (SLSQP, scipy).
5.2 · Why a risk-based method

The asset class, not the ETF count. Equal weight, risk parity 1/σ and ERC on single ETFs allocate by ETF, so the result depends on how many ETFs each asset class has: under ERC, three bond ETFs out of eight take 3/8 of the risk. Our method budgets the risk by asset class.

Estimation error. Mean–variance and maximum Sharpe maximise the in-sample Sharpe Ratio, but with means this noisy (section 04) the weights overfit: maximum Sharpe puts 35% in gold.

Regimes. Equities pay in goldilocks, bonds in a recession with falling inflation, real assets under inflation (Class 3). Fixing the risk of each basket means no regime dominates the portfolio, and the next regime is uncertain (section 01).

5.3 · Why 50 / 25 / 25

Equity is the growth engine: its premium, the best-documented long-run premium, rests on growth and earnings [16]; bonds and commodities are the brakes, in falling and rising inflation. We therefore give equity the largest share of the risk, at most 70%, and the brakes at least 30%, split equally.

Equal risk across the three baskets gives an ex-ante volatility of 7.39%, near the bottom of our band. We tested three budgets with more equity risk (Table 5) and chose 50/25/25, the closest to the 8% target on the in-sample estimate. Only then did we look at the hold-out: its Sharpe Ratios are practically equal (0.65–0.66, within the noise), while volatility and drawdown keep the in-sample ranking. The budget controls how much risk we take, not the return per unit of risk. A 60/40 portfolio has about 90% of its risk in equity, so even our 70% cap is more balanced.

6.1 · Capital versus risk

Bonds take 45% of the capital but only 25% of the risk; equity takes 31% of the capital and 50% of the risk. A dollar in equity moves the portfolio about 2.6 times as much as a dollar in bonds (volatility 15–16% against 6–7%). The capital weights therefore look defensive, but the risk is split evenly between growth and protection, by design.

6.2 · Why contributions to portfolio risk are not equal inside the baskets

Inside each basket, contributions differ because step 1 equalises risk within the basket alone, ignoring each ETF’s covariance with the other baskets; the basket totals match the budgets exactly.

6.3 · Volatility and Value at Risk

The ex-ante volatility, √(w′Vw) = 8.13%, is inside the band and close to the target. Under normality, the 1-month 5% VaR is 1.645 × 8.13% / √12 = 3.86%: in one month out of twenty we expect to lose more than USD 3.86 million. We read this as a reference, not a ceiling. In the backtest the worst month of these weights lay far beyond the normal VaR, so the true left tail is heavier. Realised volatility can also differ from 8.1%: the estimation window includes 2022, while in 2013–2021, when stocks and bonds often moved in opposite directions, the same weights had a volatility of 6.9%.

The backtest

We hold the SAA weights fixed and rebalance them to target at the end of every month. The sample is July 2013 – September 2026 (159 months): it starts when every ETF exists, the limit being BNDX (June 2013), whose index is not available to us before then. We ignore transaction costs, and count them among the reasons for the discount (CP3.4). Because the weights are estimated on the last 60 months, a single backtest would partly test the weights on the data that produced them. We therefore run three tests, each with its own question and limit:

Full sampleOur weights on all 159 months. Reference only: the last 60 months are the estimation window, so they are in-sample.
Hold-out · the test we rely onOur weights on the 99 months before the estimation window, never seen by the optimiser. No in-sample fitting, but look-ahead (2021–2026 weights on 2013–2021). It tests the mix we hold, not the method or re-optimising.
SplitThe rule estimated once on the first 60 months and held fixed on the following 99, as our SAA is held fixed. No look-ahead, but it tests the method: its weights are not ours.

7.2 · Reading the results

Return and risk. Over the full sample, USD 1 grew to USD 2.11: 5.9% a year with 7.4% volatility and a Sharpe Ratio of 0.55. The portfolio lost money in three of the twelve full calendar years (2015, 2018, 2022), never more than 9.4%.

2022, the stress test that matters. From October 2021 to September 2022 the portfolio lost 13.0%, its maximum drawdown. In 2022 US equity and US bonds fell together, so the usual hedge failed. Commodities gained and gold held roughly flat, which is the regime for which we hold them. The loss was recovered by March 2024. In March 2020 equities and commodities fell sharply, while bonds and gold held their value.

Volatility depends on the regime. With the same weights, realised volatility was 6.9% in 2013–2021, when stocks and bonds were negatively correlated, against 8.1% in the estimation window. Our weights are calibrated on the recent, less favourable regime, so they should not exceed the band if that regime persists.

The evidence is statistically weak. The t-statistics of the mean excess return are between 1.9 and 2.0, at the usual threshold of about 2: only the full sample, which is partly in-sample, just reaches it. This is the Class 2 lesson that decades of data are needed, and it is why Checkpoint 3 discounts the backtested Sharpe Ratio.

CP2.2 · Where the rules disagree with our weights

The three allocations share the same shape: every ETF holds 7–22% of the capital, all within the 7–9% band. They disagree in three places.

Equity: equal weight gives it 37.5% of the capital and 56% of the risk, because it ignores that equities are 2.6 times as volatile as bonds; risk parity gives it 24% and 42%. Our 31% of capital and exactly 50% of risk lies in between, and it is the one choice in our weights that rests on a view about returns. That view is a prior that the equity premium is the most reliable premium, not an estimated mean.

Bonds: risk parity puts 61% of the capital in the three bond ETFs because each looks safe on its own. But they are correlated 0.85–0.90 with each other and, since 2021, positively with equities, so 1/σ counts the same risk three times. Our ERC uses the correlations and holds 45%.

Real assets: risk parity cuts gold and commodities to 15% of the capital and 16% of the risk because they are volatile on their own. It ignores that they are the least correlated assets in the universe and the only ones that held up in 2022. We keep them at 25% of the risk, on the basis of correlations and our regime analysis, not their past returns.

None of our weights depends on an estimated expected return. We did not choose equal weight or risk parity 1/σ because they allocate risk ETF by ETF, not by asset class (section 5.2). The hold-out did not change our view: 1/σ had a higher Sharpe Ratio than ours (0.78 against 0.66) and equal weight a similar one (0.64), but eight years of data cannot separate Sharpe Ratios this close: each has a standard error of about 0.35 (CP3.4). Our weights rest on the design of section 05, not on backtested performance.

TuningOct 2021 – Sep 2026 (60 months): the window that sets the weights.
Hold-outJul 2013 – Sep 2021 (99 months), never used for the weights. The split test uses only past data.
VariationsUp to 33 in sample: 11 allocations on three versions of the universe, all before any backtest. The 11 of the final universe were then run once on the hold-out.

The universe changed twice, both times before any backtest. VT, used while we wrote the code, was replaced by VTI + VEA + IEMG to control the regional mix and the US concentration (section 3.1). STIP was then replaced by TIP: its 0–5 year maturities gave the bond basket a short duration, against our intermediate target, and kept the portfolio’s volatility low. In all, we had eleven portfolios in front of us: the four basket budgets we were choosing among, and the others we had committed not to choose (Checkpoint 2 benchmarks and methods we rejected on principle, section 05). In the hold-out, risk parity 1/σ beat our allocation (0.78 against 0.66) and we did not adopt it. We kept the hold-out out of the choice as far as we could: we chose the budget on the 8% target and looked at the hold-out only afterwards. It is still not fully clean, as a poor result would probably have made us revisit the choice (Class 4).

CP3.4 · The discount
Backtested Sharpe (hold-out)0.66
Realistic (discounted)0.33

We start from the hold-out, the only test of our weights on months they were not estimated on, and halve it (Class 4 rule of thumb). A raw 0.66 would overstate what we expect, for five reasons. (1) With up to 33 variations in sample, some look good by chance alone. (2) We looked at the hold-out before committing to the budget. (3) The ETFs were chosen in 2026, knowing that gold had rallied. (4) In 2013–2021 rates fell and stocks and bonds hedged each other, which no longer holds. (5) There are no trading costs. The t-statistic of 1.9 also means a standard error of about 0.35 on the Sharpe Ratio. We do not discount more because our weights have a single tuned parameter, the equity budget, and use no expected returns. This 0.33 is consistent with evidence that does not depend on our choices: in Class 3's 40-year horse race, risk parity earned 0.53 out of sample and a 60/40 portfolio 0.36.
